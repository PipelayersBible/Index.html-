Confined Space Ventilation

Not an OSHA/Cal-OSHA compliance number Neither OSHA nor Cal/OSHA sets a minimum CFM tied to space size — both require whatever forced-air ventilation it takes to pass atmospheric testing (O₂, LEL, PELs), continuously, while anyone is inside. This gives a rule-of-thumb blower size using the common 20 air-changes-per-hour guidance (ANSI Z117.1), nothing more — always confirm entry conditions with a calibrated gas meter, not this number.

Rod Units & Grade Math

Rod Types
Lenker Rod
Reads true elevation directly. No HI needed. Simplest rod, most direct number.
Engineer's Rod
Reading = HI − elevation. Decimal feet. Reading gets SMALLER as elevation gets HIGHER.
Architect's Rod
Same math as Engineer's Rod, displayed in feet-inches-1/16".
Elevation of the Instrument (HI)
Benchmark + Backsight
HI = benchmark elevation + backsight reading.
Calculate from Stakes
HI = stake elevation + rod reading shot at that stake. Works from any stake with both values.
HI cross-check tolerance
If two or more Survey Stake points imply a different HI, the app flags it: over ±0.02 ft apart is a serious warning (likely a busted setup or a reading error), 0.001–0.02 ft apart is a milder "worth a second look" flag. This shows live on the New Run screen and carries through to Review and the final report if it's never resolved.
Only needed for
Points entered as a typed Invert Elevation. A run made entirely of survey stakes never needs HI at all.
Rod Reading → Elevation calculator
Standalone tool (Calculators → Grade & Elevation) for the same HI math, outside of a run — shoot a known point, then convert any other reading to an elevation off that same HI.
Don't know the elevation, only the reading?
On the New Run screen, Invert Elevation mode has an optional "rod reading here" field — enter it instead of the elevation, and the app derives Elevation = HI − reading automatically, live, as long as this run's HI is set from somewhere.
Survey Stake Math
True invert elevation
Cut: stake elevation − cut. Fill: stake elevation + fill. This is real elevation, for documentation — the surveyor's number, unrelated to your rod.
Rod reading at invert (Engineer's/Architect's Rod)
Cut: rod reading at stake + cut. Fill: rod reading at stake − fill. This is what you actually dial in — computed directly from your own shot, with no elevation math involved at all.
BOP / INV / TOP
BOP (Bottom of Pipe)
Elevation: INV − wall thickness. Engineer's/Architect's Rod reading: INV reading + wall thickness (reads HIGHER, not lower).
TOP (Top of Pipe)
Elevation: INV + pipe ID + wall thickness. Engineer's/Architect's Rod reading: INV reading − (ID + wall thickness) (reads LOWER, not higher).
Why BOP/TOP flip direction on Engineer's/Architect's Rod
Reading = HI − elevation. Since HI is fixed, a HIGHER elevation always gives a SMALLER reading. TOP is physically highest, so it reads lowest. BOP is physically lowest, so it reads highest.
Bedding Elevation (at a structure)
Top of Bedding
Reference elevation (invert or BOP) − distance down to the bottom of the manhole/structure.
Bottom of Bedding
Top of Bedding − bedding thickness.
BOP from Invert
BOP = Invert − wall thickness, if you only have the invert reference on hand.

Pipe Cross-Section

Looking Straight Down the Pipe

Schematic, not to scale. TOP/BOP are the pipe's outside (crown/bottom); INV is the inside bottom — the number that matters for grade. Springline is the horizontal centerline.

Quick Reference
OD (Outside Diameter)
Full outer width of the pipe, wall included.
ID (Inside Diameter)
Open interior width — what "pipe size" (e.g. 12") almost always refers to. ID = OD − 2 × wall thickness.
Wall Thickness
The gap between OD and ID. (OD − ID) ÷ 2.
Springline
Horizontal line through the pipe's center — its widest point, and the standard reference line for utility conflict/clearance calls.
Circumference
π × OD — the full outer perimeter.

Slope & Grade

Slope
Slope %
(Start invert elevation − End invert elevation) ÷ Distance × 100.
Actual pipe length
√(horizontal distance² + fall²) — Pythagorean theorem. Slightly longer than the horizontal run distance on any sloped pipe.
Elevation change per interval
(Slope % ÷ 100) × interval distance. E.g. 2% slope over a 10 ft interval = 0.20 ft of fall (or rise) per interval — this is exactly how the app fills in the interval table between your points.
Percent / Ratio / Degrees
Percent → Ratio (1:X)
X = 100 ÷ percent.
Percent → Degrees
arctan(percent ÷ 100).
Ratio → Percent
(1 ÷ X) × 100.
Tenths ↔ Inches
Decimal feet → feet-inches-fraction
Whole feet stay as-is. Take the remainder × 12 for inches; the remainder of that × 16, rounded to the nearest sixteenth, for the fraction. (e.g. 3.7 ft → 3 ft, 0.7×12 = 8.4 in → 3' 8-3/8".)
Feet-inches-fraction → decimal feet
Feet + (inches ÷ 12) + (fraction ÷ 12).
Why it matters
Rod readings and elevations in this app are decimal feet — but a standard tape measures in 16ths of an inch. This conversion is what lets you actually mark a cut with the tape on your belt.

Flush / Flow Rate
GPM

C coefficients
Smooth/rounded 0.90  ·  Square/sharp 0.80  ·  Projecting into barrel 0.70  ·  Stream straightener 0.95
Material Volume
Rectangular
Length × Width × Depth
Triangular
0.5 × Base × Height × Depth

(Encasement L × W × D) − sum of all pipe cylinder volumes inside it.
Manning's Equation (full-pipe gravity flow)
Velocity
(1.49 ÷ n) × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2), where R = diameter ÷ 4 (hydraulic radius, full pipe) and S = slope as a decimal.

Velocity × cross-sectional area. This is the pipe's ceiling capacity flowing completely full — real day-to-day flow is usually partial-depth.

Net Cut/Fill & Backfill Volume (Stats/Archive)
Net cut/fill
Sum of every stake's cut or fill value across the run — cut counts positive, fill counts negative (or vice versa depending on convention), giving one overall number for how much the ground moved.
Estimated backfill volume
Trench width × distance × average cut/fill depth, converted to cubic yards. A rough estimate only — it assumes a constant trench width and vertical sides, not sloped ones, so it will under-report actual spoil volume on a sloped or benched trench.

Confined Space

Full standard text Construction confined space entry is governed by 1926 Subpart AA — separate from, and stricter in places than, the general industry standard (1910.146) many people learned on.
Ventilation
CFM (rule of thumb, not an OSHA compliance number)
(Volume in ft³ × Air Changes per Hour) ÷ 60.
ACH tiers
20 = normal (ANSI Z117.1)  ·  40 = elevated hazard  ·  60 = extreme
Attendant Rule
OSHA 1910.146(i)
The attendant must stay outside the space at all times and may not enter for any reason — including rescue — unless first relieved by another qualified attendant.
Entry Conditions
Common reference range
O2 typically 19.5%–23.5%  ·  LEL typically below 10%. These are common references only — this app's automatic evacuation alarm only activates if you enter your own site-specific limits, since crews often rely on their sniffer's built-in alarm instead.

Concrete & Slurry

Typical ranges, not a spec PSI classes and uses below are common practice, not a universal standard — the project spec, engineer's drawing, or municipal detail always governs.
Structural Concrete — PSI Classes & Typical Uses
2,500–3,000 psi
Common minimum for thrust blocks and other low-demand mass fill — poured against undisturbed soil, relying on bearing area more than raw strength.
3,000–3,500 psi
Typical for manhole bases, collars, and channel fill on most municipal specs.
3,500–4,000 psi
General structural work — footings, encasement, thrust blocks on newer/stricter specs, most everyday utility structural concrete.
4,500–5,000+ psi
Higher-demand applications — heavy structural loads, water/wastewater structures in aggressive or sulfate-heavy soils, high-traffic pavement. Often paired with a specific cement type (e.g. Type II for sulfate resistance) rather than PSI alone.
PSI is measured at 28 days
"4,000 psi concrete" means it's designed to reach 4,000 psi compressive strength 28 days after the pour — not on pour day. Early strength is much lower; respect cure time before loading it, backfilling over it, or trimming pipe inside a manhole.
Flowable Fill / Slurry (CLSM)
What it is
Controlled Low-Strength Material (CLSM) — also called flowable fill, controlled density fill, soil-cement slurry, or unshrinkable fill. A thin, self-leveling mix of cement, water, and sand or fly ash — consistency closer to pancake batter or a milkshake than concrete.
Strength
Under 1,200 psi by definition (ACI 229) — most mixes actually target under 300 psi on purpose, so the material stays easy to re-excavate later if the utility needs to be exposed again.
Purpose
Pipe bedding and trench backfill, especially narrow trenches or tight utility corridors where compaction equipment can't get in. Also used to fill abandoned pipe, void fill, and retaining wall backfill.
Why crews use it
Self-compacting — no tamping or vibration needed. Displaces standing water in a wet trench. Can often be placed without anyone entering the trench at all, which is a real cave-in exposure reduction, not just a convenience.
Trade-off
Costs meaningfully more than granular backfill per yard, and if it's mixed too strong it becomes its own future excavation headache — the whole point is low strength, more cement is not automatically better here.
Not the same as structural concrete
Flowable fill is a backfill material, not a structural one — it's not rated or intended to carry structural loads the way a footing, thrust block, or encasement pour is.

Safety Facts

This is the part worth actually reading These numbers are real, sourced from OSHA, BLS, CDC/NIOSH, and peer-reviewed research. None of it is exaggerated for effect — the real numbers are bad enough on their own.
OSHA's "Fatal Four"
Together, these four categories kill more construction workers than everything else combined
Falls, struck-by, electrocution, and caught-in/between account for over 60% of all construction fatalities every year. OSHA singled these four out specifically because fixing them has the biggest possible impact on the industry's death toll — everything else below in this section falls under one of these four.
#1 — Falls (roughly a third of all construction deaths)
The single biggest killer in construction, year after year — typically 33–38% of all deaths, more than the other three combined. Falls from roofs, ladders, scaffolds, and unprotected edges. OSHA requires fall protection at 6 ft or more above a lower level (29 CFR 1926 Subpart M). Relevant on this job mainly around excavated edges, ladders in/out of trenches, and any work on structures or embankments.
#2 — Struck-By (roughly 10–11% of all construction deaths)
Workers hit by a moving object — vehicles, swinging equipment, falling or flying material. About 75% of struck-by/run-over fatalities involve heavy equipment specifically. See "Working Around Heavy Equipment" below for the details that matter on this job.
#3 — Electrocution (roughly 8–9% of all construction deaths)
Contact with overhead power lines, underground electrical, exposed wiring, or damaged tools/cords. Silent and invisible until contact — there's no "it looked safe." Always call 811 and confirm utility locates before digging, and treat every unmarked or unverified line as live. Electrical standards: 29 CFR 1926 Subpart K.
#4 — Caught-In/Between (roughly 5–6% of all construction deaths)
Trench cave-ins, and being crushed between equipment and a fixed object (a wall, a truck, a trench wall). This is the category trench collapses fall under — see the numbers below, they're serious enough to earn their own section.
Want to read real cases, not just numbers?
NIOSH's FACE program publishes detailed, real investigation reports on fatal incidents — what happened, what went wrong, and what would've prevented it. Names of victims and employers are left out of the record out of respect. For the citation/inspection side of real incidents, OSHA's own fatality inspection database is searchable and public.
Trench Collapse
One cubic yard of soil weighs up to 3,000 lbs
As much as a small car. That's what comes down on a worker in a collapse — not "dirt," a car dropped on them in under a second.
There's no time to react
Cave-ins happen without warning and without time to climb out, even for an alert, unrestrained worker standing right next to the ladder. Death is usually from compression asphyxia — the weight crushes the chest so it can't expand, not just burial itself. Even partial burial of the torso can be fatal.
2022 was the worst year for trench deaths since 2005
39 workers died in trench collapses that year — more than double 2021. Aggressive OSHA enforcement (zero tolerance for unprotected trenches) brought it down to 15 in 2023 and 12 in 2024 — proof this is genuinely preventable, not just bad luck.
Most deaths are in trenches that were never protected at all
Not a shoring system that failed — no protective system in place to begin with. The standard is not complicated: sloping, benching, shoring, or a trench box, for any excavation 5 ft deep or more (or shallower, if a competent person sees signs of instability).
Most victims are experienced, not new guys
A lot of fatalities happen to workers who've been in the trade for years — familiarity breeds the "I've done this a hundred times without a box" mindset that gets people killed on the hundred-and-first.
Real trench collapse reports
Browse NIOSH FACE reports and filter/search for trenching or excavation incidents to read exactly how real collapses happened. OSHA's fatality database is also searchable by keyword for cited trenching cases.
Competent Person — What the Job Actually Requires
Definition (OSHA)
Someone capable of identifying existing and predictable hazards, who understands soil types and which protective systems they require, and who has the actual authority to take prompt corrective action to fix what they find (1926.650(b)).
Daily inspection, not one-and-done
Trenches, adjacent areas, and protective systems must be inspected before each shift starts, and again anytime conditions change — after rain, after a nearby vibration source shows up, after any sign of shifting or fissuring soil.
Soil classification is on them
Classifying the soil as Stable Rock, Type A, B, or C isn't a guess or a formality — it's a required judgment call that directly determines which protective system is legal to use, and it has to be based on actual testing (see below), not assumption.
Full scope of responsibility
Selecting and designing the protective system (sloping, benching, shoring, or shielding), verifying safe access/egress, monitoring for water and atmospheric hazards, keeping spoil and equipment back from the edge, and having the authority to shut work down or pull people out the moment something looks wrong.
Depth Thresholds
Source
1926.651 and 1926.652.
4 ft or more
Safe means of egress (ladder, ramp, or steps) is required, AND the atmosphere must be tested before anyone enters if there's any reason to suspect a hazardous atmosphere could be present.
5 ft or more
A protective system (sloping, benching, shoring, or shielding) is required, unless the excavation is entirely in stable rock. A competent person can require one below 5 ft too, if conditions call for it — 5 ft is a floor, not a green light.
20 ft or more
The protective system must be designed by a registered professional engineer, or based on tabulated data a P.E. has approved. This app's calculators stop at 20 ft for exactly this reason.
Ladder & Egress
The 25 ft rule
A ladder, ramp, or stairway must be positioned so no worker in the trench ever has to travel more than 25 ft laterally to reach it. In a long trench, that means multiple ladders, not one at the far end.
Extend it past the top
Standard ladder safety practice (1926.1053) — the ladder should extend at least 3 ft above the point of access so there's something solid to grab stepping off at the top, not just air.
Don't pull the ladder mid-work
If a ladder has to come out temporarily (e.g. while a trench box is being moved), OSHA has only accepted that with a documented alternate plan — a second ladder staged right at the exit point and dedicated people ready to lower it immediately. Don't leave a crew in a trench with no way out, even for a few minutes.
Field Soil Classification — How It's Actually Done
Both a visual AND a manual test, minimum
OSHA requires at least one of each — a visual test alone isn't enough to classify soil, no matter how confident the read is.
Visual test
Look at the soil and the excavation itself: grain size and texture (fine/cohesive vs. coarse/granular), signs of fissures or cracking, evidence the soil was previously disturbed, layers that dip toward the trench, and any water seeping from the sides.
Thumb penetration test (manual)
Press a thumb into a fresh, undisturbed sample. Type A: penetrates only with real effort, about ¼". Type B: sinks in to about the length of the thumbnail, roughly 1". Type C: sinks in easily, the full length of the thumb. This is the most common field test but also the least precise — OSHA notes it should ideally be paired with another method.
Dry strength test (manual)
Dry a sample out, then try to break it. Type A: breaks with real difficulty and the pieces don't crumble further. Type C: crumbles freely into individual grains with barely any pressure. Granular soil never holds together dry — that alone rules out Type A.
Plasticity / ribbon test (manual)
Roll a moist sample into a thin thread or "ribbon." If it holds together without breaking, the soil has enough clay content to be cohesive (Type A or B territory). If it won't hold a ribbon at all, it's granular — never Type A.
Pocket penetrometer (manual, numeric)
A spring-loaded gauge pushed into the soil for a direct tsf (tons per square foot) reading — roughly Type A ≥1.5 tsf, Type B 0.5–1.5 tsf, Type C <0.5 tsf. More objective than the thumb test, but OSHA itself notes these instruments carry a ±20–40% error rate — one more reason a single test is never the full picture.
When in doubt, classify it worse
If tests point to two different types, or conditions are mixed or uncertain, the standard practice is to classify the soil as the less stable of the two and protect accordingly. Guessing optimistic never has an upside here.
California — Cal/OSHA Specifics
Permit required at 5 ft, before you dig
California requires an actual Cal/OSHA permit (Title 8 CCR §341 — either a Project Permit or an Annual Permit) for any trench or excavation 5 ft or deeper that a person will descend into. This is a real permit obtained in advance from Cal/OSHA, on top of everything federal OSHA already requires — not just a paperwork formality.
Public works shoring plans
Under California Labor Code §6705, any public works contract over $25,000 involving a trench 5 ft or deeper requires the contractor to submit a detailed shoring plan to the awarding body before excavation starts.
Confined Space
About 90 workers die in confined spaces every year in the US
Roughly two a week, based on BLS fatality data.
60% of confined space deaths are the would-be rescuers
This is the single most important number in this whole section. The typical pattern: one worker goes down from a bad atmosphere. A coworker climbs in to help without testing or air — and is overcome by the exact same hazard within seconds. Sometimes a third person follows. This is exactly why the attendant is legally barred from ever entering to rescue, no matter what they're watching happen.
Atmospheric hazards are invisible and can incapacitate instantly
Most confined space deaths are from oxygen deficiency or a toxic/flammable atmosphere — undetectable without a meter, and capable of dropping a person before they even realize something's wrong. "It looked fine" has killed people.
Untested, unventilated, no rescue plan
Studies of confined space fatalities consistently find the space wasn't tested before entry and had no rescue plan in place — the two things this app's confined space workflow is built to force you through every time.
Real confined space reports
Browse NIOSH FACE reports and filter/search for confined space incidents — several document the exact "second and third victim tried to rescue" pattern described above, in real detail. OSHA's fatality database is also searchable by keyword.
Working Around Heavy Equipment
Struck-by is #2 in OSHA's "Fatal Four"
Falls, struck-by, electrocution, and caught-in/between together account for roughly 60% of all construction deaths. About 75% of struck-by/run-over fatalities involve heavy equipment specifically — excavators, dozers, loaders, trucks — not hand tools or small stuff.
Always assume the operator cannot see you
Blind spots on an excavator or loader are bigger than most people think, and a machine that size gives zero feedback that it's about to move into you. Never assume you're seen just because you can see the machine.
Get eye contact before you approach
Make eye contact with the operator and get a clear acknowledgment before entering any equipment's work area — not a wave in your general direction, actual acknowledgment that they see you specifically. If you can't see the operator's face, they can't see you either.
Stay out of the swing radius, full stop
An excavator's counterweight and boom can sweep through space you were standing in a second ago with no warning. There's no "quick dash through it" — treat the swing radius as off-limits the entire time the machine is running, not just while it's actively swinging.
Never trust a backing machine to stop for you
Equipment with an obstructed rear view is required to have a working backup alarm, or only back up when a spotter confirms it's clear — but alarms get ignored or drowned out on a loud site. Stay clear of the path, don't rely on the beep alone.
If you're the spotter
Wear high-visibility clothing, stay somewhere the operator can actually see you the entire time, use pre-established hand signals (not improvised ones), and never take your eyes off the equipment while it's moving. A spotter who looks away is not spotting.
Watch caught-between hazards, not just struck-by
Getting pinned between a moving machine and something fixed — a wall, a parked truck, a trench wall, a pipe stack — kills just as dead as being struck directly. Don't position yourself, or your tools, in that gap even briefly.
Real struck-by/caught-between reports
Browse NIOSH FACE reports and filter/search for struck-by or machinery incidents. OSHA's fatality database is also searchable by keyword for real cited cases.
The Point
None of this is rare or freak-accident stuff
These are common, well-documented, well-understood failure patterns with a known fix: use the box, slope it right, test the air, stay out of the swing radius, never go in after someone without being trained and equipped to do it. The rules exist because people already died finding out why they're there.

EDUCATION

Glossary

Grade & Survey
Benchmark (BM)
A point of known, established elevation used as a fixed reference to set up from.
Backsight (BS)
A rod reading taken on a point of known elevation, used to establish the instrument's HI.
Foresight (FS)
A rod reading taken on a point whose elevation you're trying to find.
Cut
How far down from a stake mark to the target grade.
Fill
How far up from a stake mark to the target grade.
Grade
The design elevation a point of the pipe (usually the invert) is supposed to be at.
Direct reading
What a Lenker rod gives you — the number on the rod already is the elevation, no HI math needed.
Pipe & Structure
Invert (INV)
The inside bottom of the pipe — the number that actually matters for flow and grade.
Springline
The horizontal centerline of the pipe, its widest point — the standard reference line for utility clearance calls.
Crown
The outside top of the pipe (same as TOP).
Bell / Spigot
The female (bell) and male (spigot) ends of a pipe section that join together — the spigot inserts into the bell.
Center-to-End (C-E)
Distance from a fitting's theoretical center to the internal stop shoulder, where the pipe actually seats.
Bedding
The prepared material a pipe rests on in the trench, providing uniform support along its length.
Haunching
Bedding material placed and compacted up around the lower sides of the pipe, up to the springline, for lateral support.
Lift
One layer of backfill placed and compacted before the next layer goes on top.
Cover
Depth of soil/backfill over the top of the pipe.
Trenching & Excavation
Sloping
Cutting trench walls back at an angle instead of leaving them vertical, to reduce cave-in risk.
Benching
A stepped excavation — a series of near-vertical risers and horizontal steps, instead of one continuous slope.
Shoring
A support structure (like hydraulic or timber shores) installed to prevent a trench wall from moving.
Shielding
A trench box or shield that protects workers inside it from a collapse, without necessarily preventing the collapse itself.
Competent person
Someone capable of identifying existing/predictable hazards and who has authority to take prompt corrective action — required on-site for any excavation work under OSHA.
Spoil
Excavated material piled next to the trench — must be kept at least 2 ft back from the edge.
Confined Space
Permit-Required Confined Space (PRCS)
A confined space with a hazard serious enough (atmospheric, engulfment, entrapment, or other) to require a written permit before anyone enters.
LEL (Lower Explosive Limit)
The minimum concentration of a gas in air that can ignite. Air monitors read LEL as a percentage of that threshold, not a percentage of total air.
Attendant
The person stationed outside a confined space the entire time it's occupied, tracking who's inside and never entering to rescue.
Entrant
A worker who actually enters the confined space under the permit.
Entry Supervisor
The person responsible for authorizing entry, verifying conditions, and ending entry when required.
