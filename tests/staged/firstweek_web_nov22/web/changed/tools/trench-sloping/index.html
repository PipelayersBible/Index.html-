Trench sloping calculator

How far back each wall has to lay at OSHA's maximum allowable slope, and how wide the top of the cut ends up, for excavations less than 20 feet deep.

Your competent person classifies the soil and decides the protective system. This page only does the arithmetic on OSHA's table. It doesn't classify soil, and the maximum slope is the steepest allowed, not a target.

Slope and top width

Where the numbers come from

OSHA's Table B-1 in 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, Appendix B gives the maximum allowable slopes, written horizontal to vertical (H:V), "for excavations less than 20 feet deep":

Soil or rock typeMaximum allowable slope (H:V)
Stable rockVertical (90°)
Type A3/4 : 1 (53°)
Type B1 : 1 (45°)
Type C1 1/2 : 1 (34°)

The angles are degrees from horizontal, and OSHA notes they are rounded. The math is plain:

back from the toe, each side = depth × H

top width = bottom width + 2 × depth × H

Example: 8 ft deep in Type B with a 3 ft bottom. Each wall lays back 8 ft, so the top is 3 + 8 + 8 = 19 ft wide.

What this page leaves out

  • 20 ft or deeper. Note 3 of the table: "Sloping or benching for excavations greater than 20 feet deep shall be designed by a registered professional engineer." This page gives no slope at 20 ft or more.
  • Short-term Type A. OSHA allows 1/2 : 1 (63°) in Type A only for simple slope excavations 12 feet or less deep that are open 24 hours or less. Anything deeper than 12 ft in Type A stays at 3/4 : 1. It isn't offered as a choice here.
  • Benching and vertical lower walls. Appendix B shows which benching layouts are allowed and the limits on each, and they vary by soil type (no benching layout is shown for Type C). They are not worked out here.
  • Distress and surcharge. If the walls show signs of distress, OSHA requires cutting back at least another 1/2 : 1 flatter. With spoil, equipment or traffic near the edge, the competent person decides how much flatter it has to be.
  • Shallow trenches. Federal OSHA (1926.652(a)(1)) doesn't require a protective system in stable rock, or under 5 feet deep when a competent person examines the ground and finds no indication of a potential cave-in.

State plans can be stricter

Many states run their own OSHA plan. Two examples: Washington requires a protective system starting at 4 feet instead of 5 (WAC 296-155-657), and California requires a Cal/OSHA permit before workers enter a trench or excavation 5 feet deep or more (Title 8, section 341). Check the rules for the state the job is in.

For the ladder, spoil and inspection rules that go with the depth, see trench depth rules: 4, 5 and 20 feet.
