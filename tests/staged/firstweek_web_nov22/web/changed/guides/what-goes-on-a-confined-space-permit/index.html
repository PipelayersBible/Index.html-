What goes on a confined space permit

Each item an entry permit has to cover, in plain words, with the section it comes from.

By Thomas Fontaine, pipelayer and underground foreman. November 19, 2026.

This is a plain summary to help crews read and fill out a permit. It isn't legal advice and it doesn't replace your company's permit space program, your training or your competent person. The rules are linked below; read them for the exact wording.

Which rule applies

  • Construction work (most sewer, storm drain and water work on a jobsite): federal OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart AA, Confined Spaces in Construction. The permit contents are in 1926.1206, and the permitting process is in 1926.1205.
  • General industry (for example, plant or utility maintenance that isn't construction): 29 CFR 1910.146, with the permit contents in paragraph (f).
  • California: Cal/OSHA's Confined Spaces in Construction, Title 8, Article 37 (sections 1950 to 1962). The entry permit is section 1955 and the permitting process is section 1954. Other state plans have their own versions too.

The lists line up closely. The construction rule adds one item the general industry list doesn't have: how you'll detect rising hazards if the ventilation stops.

What the permit has to identify

Under 1926.1206 (construction), the permit identifies:

  1. The permit space to be entered. Which manhole or vault, where. Use the structure number from the plans.
  2. The purpose of the entry. What work is being done in there.
  3. The date and the authorized duration. A permit doesn't run open-ended. It may not last longer than the job it covers.
  4. The authorized entrants. By name, or by a roster or tracking system that lets the attendant tell who is inside at any time.
  5. How you'll detect an increase in atmospheric hazards if the ventilation stops. For example, a continuous monitor on the entrant.
  6. Each attendant, by name.
  7. The entry supervisor, by name, and the signature or initials of each entry supervisor who authorizes entry.
  8. The hazards of the space. Low oxygen, hydrogen sulfide, methane, engulfment, traffic, whatever applies.
  9. The measures used to isolate the space and control its hazards before entry. Lockout or tagging, plugging lines, purging, ventilating, flushing.
  10. The acceptable entry conditions. The limits the air and the space have to be within for anyone to go in.
  11. The test results, with the names or initials of the testers and when the tests were done.
  12. The rescue and emergency services you can summon, and how to summon them: the equipment to use and the numbers to call.
  13. The communication procedures entrants and attendants use to stay in contact.
  14. The equipment provided: PPE, testing equipment, communications, alarms and rescue equipment.
  15. Any other information needed for this particular space to keep people safe.
  16. Any additional permits, such as a hot work permit.

Paragraph (f) of 1910.146 has the same items except number 5, and Cal/OSHA's section 1955 covers the same list for construction in California.

Air test results

OSHA's definition of a hazardous atmosphere in 1910.146(b) includes:

"Flammable gas, vapor, or mist in excess of 10 percent of its lower flammable limit (LFL)"

"Atmospheric oxygen concentration below 19.5 percent or above 23.5 percent"

29 CFR 1910.146(b), definition of hazardous atmosphere

It also covers toxic gases like hydrogen sulfide and carbon monoxide above their exposure limits. Those limits can be stricter in a state plan than under federal OSHA, so the permit's acceptable entry conditions should come from your program. Write down every reading with the time and who took it. Appendix B to 1910.146 says to test for oxygen first, then combustible gases, then toxic gases.

Signing, posting, canceling and keeping it

  • Signed before entry. The entry supervisor named on the permit signs it to authorize entry before entry begins (1926.1205(b); Cal/OSHA 1954).
  • Available at the entry. The completed permit has to be available to the entrants at the time of entry, for example posted at the entry portal (1926.1205(c); 1910.146(e)(3)).
  • Canceled when the work is done, or when a condition the permit doesn't allow comes up (1926.1205(e); 1910.146(e)(5)). Any problems during the entry get noted on the permit (1926.1205(f)).
  • Kept at least 1 year. Canceled permits are kept for at least 1 year for the review of the permit space program (1926.1205(f); 1910.146(e)(6); Cal/OSHA 1954).

Filling it out in the field

  • Fill it out at the space, not in the truck from memory.
  • Put names, not "crew." The attendant has to know exactly who is inside.
  • Write the actual rescue number and who answers it. On some sites it's an on-site rescue team, not 911.
  • Log air readings with the time and the tester's name, every time you test.
  • When something changes that the permit doesn't allow, everyone gets out, and the permit gets canceled or suspended under your program.
