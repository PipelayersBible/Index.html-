How to read a grade stake

What the writing on a pipe grade stake means, and how to get from the stake to the invert.

By Thomas Fontaine, pipelayer and underground foreman. November 19, 2026.

Every surveyor marks stakes a little differently, so treat this as the common way of doing it, not the only way. When a stake doesn't make sense, check the cut sheet's notes or call the surveyor before you dig to it.

The hub and the guard stake

A grade stake is usually two pieces. The hub is a short stake driven down near the ground, often with a tack or a mark on top. The top of the hub is the point the surveyor shot, and every number on the stake is measured from it. The guard stake (the lath) stands next to it so you can find the hub and read the writing.

Don't knock the hub or set anything on it. If it moves, its numbers are no good.

What's written on it

  • Station. Distance along the line, written in hundreds of feet: 1+25.00 is 125 feet from the start of the line (0+00).
  • Offset. How far the stake is from the pipe centerline, and often the line type. "10' O/S" or "10/SS" means 10 feet off the centerline of the sewer. The number in a tag like 10/SS is the offset, not the pipe size. Offsets can change along a line when stakes get moved around an obstruction.
  • Line type. SS sanitary sewer, SD storm drain, W water, and so on.
  • Cut or fill. C 6.45 means the point you're building to is 6.45 feet below the top of the hub. F 1.20 means it's 1.20 feet above the hub.
  • What the cut is to. Usually the invert or flowline (field crews use the two words for the same thing), sometimes the top of pipe or a structure rim. If it doesn't say, ask.
  • Back of the stake. The stake number and the hub elevation are often written on the back. Read both sides.

At structures you'll see the name, like MH-3, with an IE IN and an IE OUT. IE IN is the invert of the pipe coming in, the higher side; IE OUT is the pipe going out, the lower side.

If a number is crossed out or scribbled over, it's the surveyor's mistake. Don't use it. If you can't tell which number is the good one, call.

Turning the cut into an invert elevation

If you know the hub elevation, the invert is simple:

invert = hub elevation − cut    (or + fill)

Example: the back of the stake says the hub is 102.48. The front says C 6.45 to the invert. The invert at that station is 102.48 − 6.45 = 96.03.

The cut sheet lists the same thing for every stake: stake number, station, offset, hub elevation, design invert and the cut or fill. Cut is just hub elevation minus design invert, so you can check any line on the sheet with a subtraction.

Cuts in feet and inches

Stakes are in feet and hundredths. If your tape is in feet and inches, convert the decimal part: multiply it by 12 for inches, then multiply what's left over by 16 for sixteenths.

6.45 feet: 0.45 × 12 = 5.4 inches. 0.4 × 16 = 6.4, call it 6/16, which is 3/8. So C 6.45 is 6'-5 3/8".

Grade between the stakes

Stakes might be 25 or 50 feet apart, and you need grade everywhere in between. On a straight grade the invert changes the same amount every foot:

change = slope % ÷ 100 × distance

At 0.40%, the invert drops 0.004 feet per foot, so 0.10 feet every 25 feet. Work the slope out from two stakes and compare it with the plans. If the two don't agree, something is off: a typo on the sheet, a disturbed hub or a misread number. Sort it out before you set pipe.

Quick checks before you lay pipe

  • Read both sides of every stake.
  • Make sure you know what the cut is to: invert, top of pipe or rim.
  • Check the slope between two stakes against the plans.
  • Shoot a second hub to check your instrument setup.
  • Ignore anything crossed out, and call when in doubt.
